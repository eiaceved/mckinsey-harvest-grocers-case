01

How a McKinsey-style case works

In a McKinsey-style case, the interviewer leads. You get one question at a time: structure the problem, read a chart, do some math, brainstorm, then give a recommendation.

Put simply, they want to see you think in a clear structure, stay calm with numbers, and land on an answer a client could act on. Formats vary by office, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Many candidates first take Solve, a game-based online test.
  • Each interview pairs a case of about 30 minutes with a Personal Experience Interview of about 10 to 15 minutes, where you tell one story in depth.
  • Usually two interviews in the first round, then two or three in the final round.

What they look for

The same skills in every question

  • Structure: clear buckets with no overlaps and no gaps.
  • Numbers: clean mental math, said out loud.
  • Creativity: many sensible ideas, grouped into themes.
  • Communication: the answer first, then the reasons.

What the case feels like

  • Framework. "What would you look at to decide?" Take a minute, then walk through three or four buckets.
  • Exhibit. A chart appears. Find the one or two things that matter, and say why.
  • Math. A short calculation with round numbers. Talk through every step.
  • Brainstorm. "What else could the client do?" Give ideas in groups, not a random list.
  • Synthesis. The CEO walks in. Give the answer, the reasons, the risk, and the next step.

The shape of the case

  1. StructureBreak the problem into parts.
  2. AnalyzeCharts and math.
  3. BrainstormIdeas, grouped.
  4. RecommendAnswer first.

The interviewer moves you from one question to the next

Ten terms to drop naturally

MECE

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Your buckets don't overlap, and together they cover everything that matters.

Hypothesis

Your best guess at the answer, stated early, then tested with data. It shows you're steering, not wandering.

Unit economics

The profit of one unit of the business, like one clinic or one store, before you multiply it out.

Fixed vs. variable costs

Fixed costs stay the same no matter how busy you are, like salaries. Variable costs grow with each sale, like supplies.

Break-even

The point where revenue exactly covers costs. Below it you lose money; above it you make money.

Payback period

How long it takes for profits to repay the upfront cost. A 400 thousand dollar clinic earning 200 thousand a year pays back in 2 years.

Ramp-up

The time a new location needs to reach its normal level of business. Early months always look weaker.

Halo effect

Extra value a new business creates for the rest of the company, like clinic patients filling prescriptions at the store's pharmacy.

Pilot

A small test before a full rollout. Its results are your best evidence, as long as the test stores look like the rest.

Synthesis

Pulling everything into one clear recommendation: the answer, the reasons, the risk, and the next steps.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Harvest Grocers, a supermarket chain with about 400 stores across the Midwest and about 10 billion dollars in yearly sales. Like most grocers, it keeps only about 2 cents of profit for every dollar it sells.

Harvest's CEO wants new ways to grow and to bring shoppers in more often. One idea is to open small walk-in health clinics inside its stores, run by nurse practitioners, for things like flu shots, sore throats, and school physicals. Harvest tested 10 clinics over the past year.

The CEO has asked us: should Harvest roll out in-store clinics, and if so, how?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So the CEO wants to know whether Harvest should roll out in-store health clinics beyond the pilot, and if so, how big and how fast. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes, exactly." Restating confirms the objective, shows you listened, and buys you a few seconds to think.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What's the main goal: clinic profit, or more shopper visits?
    Both. Each clinic should make money on its own within three years, and the CEO also wants more pharmacy and grocery trips.
  • What did each pilot clinic cost to open?
    About 400 thousand dollars to build. We'll share the pilot results in a moment.
  • Who are the competitors?
    Urgent care centers and a few drugstore clinics. About a third of Harvest stores have a clinic within 5 miles.
  • How many stores could host a clinic?
    About 150 stores have the space and few clinics nearby.
  • Any constraints?
    Up to 60 million dollars over three years. Harvest has never run a health business, so it would need to hire nurse practitioners and a medical director.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like what color the clinics will be. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Great. What factors would you look at to decide whether Harvest should roll out the clinics?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch three or four buckets, then walk the interviewer through them out loud.

Strong framework

Should Harvest roll out in-store clinics, and how?
1Customers and market
  • Which services shoppers want
  • Competition near each store
  • How often people would come
2Clinic economics
  • Visits × revenue per visit
  • Fixed vs. variable costs
  • Break-even and payback
3Benefits to the store
  • Pharmacy prescriptions
  • Extra grocery trips
  • Loyalty and brand
4Ability to win and risks
  • Hiring nurse practitioners
  • Insurance billing and rules
  • Rollout pace, where to start

Say it like this

"I'd look at four things. First, customers and the market: what services shoppers would use, and how much competition each store faces. Second, the economics of one clinic: visits, revenue per visit, costs, and how long until it pays back. Third, the wider benefit to Harvest, like extra prescriptions and grocery trips, since that's part of the CEO's goal. Fourth, whether Harvest can run clinics well, and the risks. My early hypothesis is that clinics make sense in some stores, not all of them, so I'd like to start with what shoppers want and what the pilot showed."

Why it works

  • It's built for this case. A generic list like "market, competition, costs" would miss the pharmacy benefit.
  • The buckets don't overlap and they flow: demand, then economics, then wider value, then feasibility.
  • It ends with a hypothesis and a place to start, which hands the interviewer a natural next step.

If you blank, "customers, economics, capabilities, risks" works for almost any new-business case.

05

Exhibit 1: what shoppers want

Interviewer

Good. Before the pilot, Harvest surveyed 2,000 of its shoppers. What stands out to you?

Which finding should you lead with?

Popular isn't the same as valuable. Flu shots draw the most interest, but they're cheap, about 40 dollars, and mostly happen in the fall. Minor illness visits pay more and are popular, at 41 percent. Checkups for blood pressure or diabetes pay the most, but only 12 percent are interested. So expect busy flu seasons and revenue per visit below 100 dollars. The upside: flu shots are a cheap way to bring people in the first time.

Then

Say the insight out loud, tie it to the decision, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"The biggest draw is flu shots, at 62 percent, but they're cheap and seasonal. Minor illness visits are the real money-maker people want, at 41 percent. Higher-value checkups have little interest so far. So I'd expect busy winters, slower summers, and revenue per visit below 100 dollars. I'd like to see how the pilot clinics actually did."

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the client, then a hypothesis-driven ask. That ask leads the interviewer to Exhibit 2.

06

Exhibit 2: the pilot results

Interviewer

Here's what the 10 pilot clinics did over their first year. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Visits grew from 10 to 22 a day and are still rising. Revenue per visit is creeping up but sits around 90 dollars, below 100, which matches the survey. Staff costs are flat at 125 thousand a season, so every extra visit drops almost straight to profit. And about half of patients fill a prescription in the store, which is value the clinic's own numbers don't show.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"The pilot is working: visits are up from 10 to 22 a day and patients rate it 4.7 out of 5. But revenue per visit is about 90 dollars and staff costs are fixed, so a clinic only makes good money if visits keep climbing toward 25 a day. The hidden prize is the pharmacy, since half of patients fill a prescription in the store, so I'd like to work out one clinic's profit, including that benefit."

Clinic math: is one clinic worth it?

This is unit economics: work out the profit of one clinic, then multiply it out. State the formula, use round numbers, and sanity-check the result.

Interviewer

Your move

Say the formula first: profit equals visits times revenue per visit, minus variable and fixed costs. Then add the pharmacy benefit.

  1. 1 · Revenue per clinic25 × $90 × 360$810K
  2. 2 · Costs25 × $20 × 360 = $180K, + $500K fixed$680K
  3. 3 · Clinic profit$810K − $680K$130K
  4. 4 · Break-even visits per day$500K ÷ ($70 × 360). The pilot is at 22≈20
  5. 5 · Add the pharmacy benefit25 × 50% × $15 × 360 ≈ $70K. Total ≈ $200K$200K
  6. 6 · Payback and scale$400K ÷ $200K = 2 years. 150 clinics → $30M a year for $60M2 yrs

Quick check: about how many visits a day does a clinic need to break even?

500 thousand of fixed costs, divided by 70 dollars kept per visit times 360 days, is about 20 visits a day. Everyday version: if a lemonade stand costs 100 dollars to set up and you keep 1 dollar per cup, you need 100 cups to break even.

Clinic profit = $130K a year Break-even ≈ 20 visits a day With pharmacy ≈ $200K, 2-year payback
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"A mature clinic makes about 130 thousand dollars a year on its own, and about 200 thousand once you add pharmacy profit, so the 400 thousand dollar build pays back in about 2 years. Break-even is about 20 visits a day and the pilot is at 22, so there's not much room for error. Across 150 good-fit stores, that's about 30 million a year for 60 million invested."

If the interviewer asks what happens at 20 visits a day instead of 25: "The clinic itself just breaks even, but the pharmacy still adds about 55 thousand a year, so it's still positive. Payback stretches to about 7 years, which is why I'd set a visit target before expanding."

08

Brainstorm, then prioritize

Interviewer

The CEO wants more patients in every clinic. What could Harvest do to get there? Brainstorm out loud first. Then score the team's six ideas for ease and impact, from 1 to 5. Which three would you do first?

Your move

Brainstorm in two or three groups for 60 seconds, then score the six ideas and name your top three.

Strong brainstorm, grouped

Reach shoppers Harvest already has

Book visits in the Harvest app with loyalty points. Promote at checkout and on receipts. Have pharmacists suggest the clinic.

Find new patient groups

Sign up local employers for flu shots and physicals. Partner with schools and sports leagues. Work with health plans for seniors.

Give more reasons to visit

Add blood pressure and diabetes checkups. Offer basic lab tests. Add after-hours telehealth.

Make visits easier

Online check-in so there's no wait. Longer evening and weekend hours. Clear prices posted up front.

Groups make you sound organized and help you find ideas you'd otherwise miss.

Then

Give one reason for each score, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with three. App booking with loyalty points, because most shoppers already use the app. Checkout and receipt promotion, because it's nearly free. And employer partnerships, because each deal brings in a group of patients at once. Chronic care checkups are the biggest prize, but they need insurance contracts, so I'd make them phase two."

    Which three come first?

    A, B, and C score highest. They're cheap or quick and bring in visits soon, which matters because the clinics sit close to break-even. D, chronic care, has the most impact but is hard, so make it phase two once the basics work. E adds little. F, opening everywhere at once, is the riskiest idea on the list.

    09

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO just walked in. You have 60 seconds. What's your recommendation?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Harvest should roll out clinics, but in phases: about 150 stores over three years, starting with 30 next year, not all 400."

    "First, shoppers want it, and the pilot proves it. Visits climbed from 10 to 22 a day, and patients rate it 4.7 out of 5."

    "Second, the money works in the right stores. A mature clinic earns about 200 thousand dollars a year including pharmacy profit, so the 400 thousand dollar build pays back in about 2 years. Across 150 stores, that's about 30 million a year for 60 million invested, which fits the budget."

    "Third, start where it's easiest to win: stores with space and no clinic within 5 miles. Then push visits with app booking, checkout promotion, and employer deals."

    "The main risk is that visits stall near break-even, about 20 a day. So I'd set a gate: keep expanding only if new clinics reach 20 visits a day by month 9. Next steps: pick the first 30 stores, launch app booking, and start insurance talks for chronic care as phase two."

    The rollout plan

    Year 1Prove
    • Open 30 clinics in the best-fit stores.
    • Launch app booking and checkout promotion.
    • Gate: 20 visits a day by month 9.
    Year 2Expand
    • Open 60 more clinics.
    • Sign employer partnerships in each city.
    • Start insurance contracts for chronic care.
    Year 3Scale
    • Open the last 60 clinics.
    • Launch chronic care checkups in top clinics.
    • Review the other 250 stores for a next wave.

    Indicators to track

    • Visits per clinic per day. Target: 25 by month 18.
    • Revenue per visit. Target: 95 dollars or more.
    • Patients filling a prescription in-store. Target: half or more.
    • Payback per clinic. Target: 2 and a half years or less.

    Bonus drills: market sizing and growth

    Two short reps. McKinsey interviewers sometimes ask a sizing question on its own.

    Market sizing: flu shots

    Interviewer

    Estimate how many flu shots are given in the U.S. each year, and what they're worth.

    Your move

    Go top-down. Start with the population, narrow to people who get a shot, then multiply by price. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Start broadU.S. population≈330M
    2. × 45% get a shot each yearOne shot per person per year≈150M
    3. × $40 per shot150M × $40≈$6B
    4. Harvest's slice150 clinics × 15 a day × 90 days≈200K
    5. Share200K ÷ 150M≈0.1%
    6. Sanity checkAt most one shot per person, and many skip it✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Market growth: retail health care

    Interviewer

    Is retail health care a growing business? What would make you more or less confident?

    Your move

    Split growth into more patients and more spend per patient, then list drivers and headwinds.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more patients × more spend per patient
    Hard-to-get appointments

    Long waits to see a doctor push people toward walk-in options.

    Convenience

    Evenings, weekends, and no appointment, in a store people already visit.

    Aging population

    More people with conditions that need regular checkups.

    Employers

    Companies want cheaper, faster care options for their workers.

    Headwinds

    • Some large retailers have closed clinic businesses in recent years because visits and insurance payments didn't cover costs.
    • Telehealth apps compete for simple visits.
    • Nurse practitioners are hard to hire and expensive.

    Quick math

    If visits per clinic grow 10 percent a year, how long until they double? Use the rule of 72: 72 divided by 10 is about 7 years.

    "Demand is growing, but the business is hard to make profitable, and some big players have pulled out. That's why I'd grow in phases, with clear visit targets, and lean on the pharmacy benefit that a standalone clinic doesn't get."
